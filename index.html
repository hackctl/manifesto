Why rebuild what already works?

1. Good software is already out there

Great software is already out there, equipped with great functionality. However, it is usually tailored for general use, and its functionality often gets hidden by the complexity it carries in terms of configuration, operations, setup, and so on. Though the software is battle-tested and built with great quality, people often hop between different versions or variants of it to get the functionality right, when that functionality was always present.

Building your own software from scratch to avoid this is tempting, but it creates a great liability and danger, because what you build is not always battle-tested and not always trusted.

Secondly, code is a liability. Even after you have built a system, you always need to maintain it. That can be justified, but in most cases, we may not even need it.

Simplicity is complex enough. If we tried to make the software simple, it would become a wrapper, and that was never our idea.

2. Use software to the fullest

The software is fine. What slows it down is the road: how it is set up, configured, and operated.

Traffic, meaning the systems and users that depend on that software, is already running on the road, and it is always there. We do not add a layer to the existing infrastructure, and we do not tear the road up. Instead, we build a bridge next to it: a composable subsystem that eases the traffic on the original road.

  1. We divert the traffic. The software is already in use, so we build a new bridge and divert traffic to it accordingly. The software's hidden functionality becomes reachable through it.
  2. The bridge is not the road. It can be open, blocked, or dropped, whatever anyone likes. We never touch the original software or its setup. It stays intact, so existing systems keep working.
  3. We leave the speed breakers alone. We do not remove the software's built-in limits and safeguards. They slow the traffic, but they keep it safe, and the original road keeps working exactly as it did.
  4. We only work on the bridge. All changes and extensions happen on the new bridge. A lane connects the two, so traffic can be diverted or used with ease.

3. How hackctl solutions are made accessible to everyone

The hackctl solution, open to anyone who needs it, with the right path for each kind of user.

The road is open to everyone. hackctl is for everyone, from solo developers and small teams to large enterprises, and the bridge is open to all of them.

Open does not mean unmanaged. A bridge with no lanes, no traffic lights, and no one directing traffic is simple, but it is also chaos. So the bridge has a different path for each kind of traveler: one for pedestrians, one for cyclists, one for heavy vehicles, and one for everything else. These are the different types of customers hackctl might have, from a solo developer on foot to an enterprise moving heavy loads. The lanes are kept intact, the traffic is managed, the lights are there, and the cop is there. We open the road to more people without removing what keeps the traffic safe and moving.

4. When we need boats, we build one

Sometimes no existing software fits the need.

Some places have no road, and nothing on land will carry you across water. When we do need a boat, we build from scratch. Even then, we study and borrow battle-tested approaches and trusted functionality, and we follow the rest of what has already proven itself. Like a design system, we compose small, proven pieces into something cohesive instead of redesigning every piece ourselves.

In short

We don't reinvent software. We make sure whatever you carry gets across, reliably.
